Power of two hash tables are quietly collapsing your bucket array.

Power-of-two table sizing trades hashing entropy for single-cycle bitwise masking.

Picture this:
→ Sizing your hash table capacity to powers of two for speed
→ Inserting memory pointers or IDs that increment with a fixed stride
→ Watching ninety percent of your buckets sit completely empty

Compilers and runtimes love sizing hash tables to exact powers of two. Division on modern x86 and ARM processors is notoriously slow, costing between ten and twenty clock cycles. A bitwise AND against capacity minus one drops that index mapping cost to a single cycle.

That speed comes with a trap. A bitwise mask only reads the lowest bits of the hash. If your keys are memory addresses with 64-byte cache alignment or database IDs generated in fixed steps, the lowest bits are consistently zero or share common factors with your capacity.

When the stride and the table size share powers of two, the hash collapses into a tiny fraction of available buckets. Your constant-time lookup degrades into an O(N) collision scan that blows out CPU cache lines.

Apply a bit mixer like SplitMix to scramble your bits before masking, or ditch power of two sizing for prime-number capacities using Lemire reduction.
