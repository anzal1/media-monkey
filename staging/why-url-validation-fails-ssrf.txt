Your URL validator checked out clean.
Your fetch went through anyway.
Your metadata tokens just left the building. 🔓

DNS rebinding is the reason you cannot validate an outbound URL and then fetch it in a separate step.

Picture this:
→ Your app lets users enter a webhook URL or an avatar link.
→ Your validator resolves the host, sees a public IP, and returns true.
→ Your HTTP client makes the real request half a millisecond later.

Between that validation check and the real connection, the attacker flips the answer. Their DNS server returns a TTL of zero. The initial check gets a benign public address like 1.1.1.1, so your guard lets it pass. But when your HTTP client opens the socket, the cache is already empty. It resolves the host again, and this time the attacker hands back 169.254.169.254.

Your HTTP client connects straight to the internal instance metadata service. It dumps IAM role credentials, internal endpoints, and VPC network maps right back into the response payload.

Redirects punch the same hole. If the target server responds with a 302 redirect toward an internal endpoint and your client follows it, your initial URL validation did literally nothing.

The 2019 Capital One breach compromised over 100 million customer accounts and cost over 80 million dollars in fines using this exact vector.

Stop separating the check from the fetch. Resolve the host once, validate the resolved IP against private IP ranges like 10.0.0.0/8 and 169.254.0.0/16, and dial that exact IP directly while setting the Host header manually.

Remember this before you write your next webhook worker.
