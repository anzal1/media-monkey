A 70B model needs 140GB in float16.
Your single H100 only holds 80GB.
Applying weight quantisation drops the footprint to 35GB without retraining 📦

Quantisation maps high-precision floating point parameters into compact low-bit integer buckets.

Here is where it bites:
→ Uniform rounding squashes subtle weights into zero
→ Emergent feature outliers blow out linear scale factors
→ A bad 4-bit scheme destroys generation coherence

Every parameter starts life as a 16-bit decimal value. To run inference cheaper, you find the maximum absolute value across a group of weights and calculate a scale factor. That factor divides continuous math into 256 discrete bins for int8, or 16 bins for int4.

Because token generation is bottlenecked on reading weights from VRAM into compute units on every single pass, halving the byte size cuts required memory bandwidth in half. That means higher generation throughput on smaller hardware.

The danger is outlier channels. In models over 6.7B parameters, certain activation dimensions spike to extreme magnitudes. Naive uniform scaling across an entire layer stretches the range, flattening normal weights and tanking model perplexity. Modern runtimes use per-channel activation scaling or quantise all linear layers in groups of 128 weights with error compensation to keep accuracy intact.
