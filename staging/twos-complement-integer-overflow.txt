Two's complement is the binary format processors use to represent signed integers without building a separate subtraction circuit.

Here is where it bites:
→ Hitting 2,147,483,648 views on a video counter
→ Generating a random positive integer via abs(rand())
→ Validating array indices before passing them to a buffer

Because zero takes up one of the positive bit patterns, thirty-two bit signed integers range from -2,147,483,648 to +2,147,483,647. There is simply no positive twin for INTMIN.

When you flip the bits of INTMIN and add one, the arithmetic logic unit wraps the result straight back around into the sign bit. On paper it looks like a math trick. In hardware, it leaves the exact same negative number in your register.

In C and C++, signed overflow is undefined behavior. Modern optimizing compilers know this, assume overflow never happens, and will delete your error-checking code entirely.

Use unsigned types for pure bit manipulation, enable compiler sanitizers in your test pipeline, and handle INTMIN before calling absolute value.

Next time your telemetry counter wraps backwards, look at the bit width.
